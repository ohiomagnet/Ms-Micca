Vol. 01
The P.E.E. Framework
The Framework

Three sentences, in order.

Ms Micca said the same thing every lesson: point, evidence, explanation. Say what you think. Prove it with the text. Then say why the proof proves the thought. Below — in her words.

— 01 —
P

Point

Make a claim. One sentence. Arguable. Not a fact about the text — a position on it. If a reasonable person could disagree, you have a point. If they couldn't, you have a summary.

Steinbeck presents Curley's wife as lonely before she is dangerous. — a point

— 02 —
E

Evidence

A quote from the text. Short. Honest. Embedded into your own sentence so it reads like one breath — never dropped in cold, never longer than it needs to be. Pick the words that do work.

She stood "looking in" at the men, her voice "soft." — the evidence

— 03 —
E

Explanation

Connect them. Why does this evidence prove this point? Zoom in on the verb, the adjective, the punctuation. Then zoom out — what does it mean for the whole text, the whole character, the whole argument?

The verb "looking in" places her outside the room before it places her outside the law — she is excluded before she is feared. — the explanation

A Worked Example

One paragraph, broken open.

A paragraph built with P.E.E. On the left, the passage we were reading — it stays with you as you scroll. On the right, the three moves that made the paragraph work, annotated the way Ms Micca would mark up our essays.

The Passage

She was suddenly apprehensive. The wind had risen. She leapt — and the moor took her, the way the moor takes everything that stands still long enough.

Anon. · The Moor · Chapter IV
Point

The writer presents the moor as a force that does not merely threaten the protagonist — it absorbs her.

A single claim. Arguable. Someone else could read the moor as merely atmospheric; this paragraph insists otherwise. That insistence is what makes it a point, not a summary.

Evidence

The narrator tells us "the moor took her, the way the moor takes everything that stands still long enough."

The quotation is embedded — it reads as part of the writer's own sentence. The verb takes is the load-bearing word, so it is kept inside the quotation marks, where it belongs. Nothing is dropped in cold.

Explanation

The verb "takes" does two things at once — it implies theft (the moor steals her) and inevitability (the moor takes everything). By repeating it across both clauses, the writer turns a single moment of fear into a statement about the whole landscape.

Zoom in on the word. Then zoom out: the repetition is what makes the moor feel like a slow, patient collector of lives. That zoom-in-then-out is the explanation doing its job — and it is the part Ms Micca always circled twice in red pen.
